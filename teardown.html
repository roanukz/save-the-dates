The short version

Summary

  • The problem is real, documented and durable. Google Photos Takeout strips capture date and GPS into JSON sidecars, and Google's late-2024 rename to .supplemental-metadata.json broke the leading free tool. The position is genuinely empty: a free, non-technical, 100% client-side, browser-based repairer.
  • The product judgment is in the costly decisions, not the code. GPS-derived timezone (a documented reversal of the simpler plan), JPEG-only scope with safe passthrough, two deliberately-hard-to-confuse CSV reports, and privacy you can verify rather than take on trust.
  • The binding constraint is distribution, not capability. With one commit and no users, this cannot support market-share claims, and this teardown does not make any. The top three roadmap items are all distribution, not features. That ordering is the point.

How this was built

This tool was built with AI assistance (Claude Code). I am a product person, not a career engineer, and I am deliberate about that: my contribution is the product judgment, not the syntax. I chose the client-side architecture to make privacy verifiable, reversed the original timezone plan in favour of the harder GPS-derived approach because travel photos are the memories people most care about, scoped to JPEG-first with safe passthrough so users would not have to pre-sort, and isolated Google's volatile matching rules in one function because the export format keeps breaking other tools. The AI wrote code against those decisions; the decisions are mine.

Part A

The problem

When someone leaves Google Photos, they use Google Takeout to export their library. The photos come back, but the when and the where do not come back inside the photos. Google stores the original capture time and GPS position in separate JSON sidecar files rather than writing them into each JPEG's EXIF. Most photo apps — Apple Photos, Lightroom, Immich, a NAS — read EXIF, not sidecars, so every photo lands with the wrong date, usually the export date, and no location. A library's timeline collapses.

Framed in Jobs To Be Done (Clayton Christensen; the hire/fire formulation and the milkshake study from Competing Against Luck, 2016), the user is not hiring a "metadata tool." The functional job is: when I move my photo library off Google, help me keep my memories in the right time and place so my new app shows my life in order.

The emotional and social dimensions are what give the job its weight. These are birthdays, trips, and a child growing up, and a scrambled timeline feels like losing the memory itself. The firing event is concrete and dated: a user runs an export, imports it, sees everything stamped with today's date, and starts searching for a fix.

The forces of progress

ForceWhat it looks like here
PushThe end of free unlimited Google Photos storage — announced 11 November 2020, effective 1 June 2021, citing 4 trillion photos stored and 28 billion uploaded weekly — triggered mass migrations. Account bans and lock-in fear push others.
PullSelf-hosted destinations (Immich) and rival clouds are attractive.
Anxiety"Will a tool corrupt my originals? Do I have to trust a website with my photos and their GPS locations?"
Habit"It is easier to just keep paying Google One."

Save the Dates is designed against those forces. It never touches originals, which reduces anxiety. It runs entirely in the browser with no upload, which reduces the privacy anxiety that is unusually high here because the data is literally a map of where you have been. And it is free with no install, which lowers the switching cost that habit exploits.

Part B

Evidence the gap is real and recurring

This is not a hypothesised problem. It recurs across independent users, communities and years.

  • Adobe Community — Nov 2019, continuing into 2020
    "Photo exports have wrong dates." Early documentation that Takeout dates import wrong.
  • Samsung Community — May 2021
    Tied to the 1 June 2021 end of free unlimited storage, which triggered mass migrations.
  • Adobe — Jul 2022
    "Can't open the json photos." Users do not even understand what the sidecar files are.
  • Immich Discussion #5083 — 2023
    Many Immich users are migrating from Google Photos via Google Takeout which uses JSON sidecars… Currently many of us are using scripts or writing small programs to extract this data and edit the exif.
    Maintainers declined to build it in, pointing users to the third-party immich-go tool. Discussion #21392 raised the same request again for the new format.
  • Google Photos Takeout Helper #353 — 2024–2025
    Documents the late-2024 rename to .supplemental-metadata.json that broke existing tools. User report, verbatim:
    I have some that are dated to 1868 and 2068.
  • Ente #4953 — 2025
    A second self-hosted destination hit by the same rename, with some exports using old names and others new.
  • Apple Community thread 256157477 — Oct 2025
    meta data of all files set to the takeout date, and hence of no use anymore
    The same thread confirms the timezone problem: Google Photos appears to use UTC rather than local time.
  • Hacker News — Mar 2024, Jun 2024
    Item 40700146, Why Google Takeout is sooo bad (144 points), and the Takeout metadata complaints running through item 39570692, Ente: Open-Source, E2E Encrypted, Google Photos Alternative (964 points).
  • ExifTool forum & Lightroom Queen — various
    Topics 11064, 16079, 14771. Google writes metadata in its own proprietary JSON rather than industry-standard sidecars.

Freshness check, late 2025 into 2026

The problem is still live and, if anything, worse. The late-2024 rename is still rolling out inconsistently, so a single export can contain the old format, the new format, or both. Google also truncates filenames at 46 characters, producing a slot machine of truncated sidecar names — .supplemental-metadat.json, .supple.json, .s.json. Guides updated in March and April 2026 still describe the wrong-date problem as current. There is no evidence Google has fixed the export behaviour.

This is a durable, recurring, independently verified problem, and the format keeps changing in ways that break tools. That last fact is central to the product thesis — see decision 5.

Part C

Why the incumbent does not serve it

Google could re-embed metadata into files at export time. It does not. The honest framing is incentive analysis, not accusation — I am interpreting motive here, so I flag it as inference rather than proven intent.

1. Storage economics

Google does not keep your original file intact. As described in the Apple Community thread, Google strips out the metadata and saves it elsewhere, then reformats the files to save storage cost. Re-assembling a faithful original at export is extra engineering work on data Google deliberately disaggregated to cut cost. There is no upside for Google.

2. Retention friction

A software engineer who documented his own 263 GB extraction argued the friction is a strategy: every one of these friction points exists because Google calculated that most people will give up and keep paying for Google One. Friction on the way out protects subscriptions.

The load-bearing claim is the weaker one

There is no investigative-journalism proof of deliberate malice, and one cited engineer conceded it is unlikely there was any ill intent. So the claim I am actually making is smaller and more defensible: Google has no incentive to fix it, therefore the gap persists. That is enough to explain an unsolved problem this old.

This is the structural-moat point. The gap is not a Google oversight that Google will close next quarter and crush a small tool. It is a stable consequence of Google's cost structure and retention incentives. The opportunity is durable precisely because the incumbent is structurally uninterested.

Part D

What I built

Save the Dates is a single-page, 100% client-side browser tool. You open index.html; that is the entire install. No server, no build step, no package manager, no accounts, no database, no analytics, no telemetry, and no network calls at runtime. It reads Takeout JSON sidecars and writes the original capture date and GPS position back into each JPEG's EXIF, then hands back a new copy as a download. It never modifies originals.

I use Marty Cagan's four product risks (Inspired, SVPG: value, usability, feasibility, viability) as the spine, because each interesting decision retires a specific risk.

Decision 1

GPS-derived timezone — a documented reversal

The original plan recommended the simpler approach: convert the UTC timestamp to the browser's local timezone, disclose it in the UI, and file GPS-derived inference as a future enhancement. I reversed this and built the harder, more correct version — derive the timezone from the photo's GPS position using tz-lookup, not from the machine running the tool. A photo taken at 3pm in London reads 3pm whether the tool runs at home or abroad. Daylight saving is resolved for the actual date via Intl. Photos with no GPS fall back to UTC, exactly as Google stored it. The tool also writes the OffsetTime* tags so apps that understand them can recover the true instant.

  • CostAn extra vendored dependency and added complexity.
  • BoughtCorrect times for travel photos — disproportionately the memories people care about, and the emotional core of the job.
  • WhyThe browser-local approach fails silently and worst precisely for the highest-value photos, and the failure stays invisible until much later.

Under Cagan, this is a deliberate acceptance of higher feasibility cost to retire a value risk. It is the decision I would point to first: a costly commitment to correctness against the easier path, with the tradeoff stated rather than hidden.

Decision 2

JPEG-only scope, with non-JPEG passthrough

HEIC, PNG and video are counted, listed and left strictly alone, so you do not have to sort a mixed folder before using this. That is a usability decision disguised as a scope decision — the alternative imports a chore into a tool whose whole point is removing a chore.

Under the Kano model (Kano et al., 1984), silently and safely passing files through is close to a must-be expectation once you decide not to process them; if the tool choked or lost files, users would be furious. Counting and listing them is a small performance attribute: it proves nothing was dropped.

Decision 3

Two reports: preview versus results

A PREVIEW report of what will be written, and a RESULTS report of what was. They are deliberately hard to confuse, since one describes photos nothing has happened to yet.

Under Kano this is a delighter. No competitor offers it, most users would not have asked for it, and its absence would generate no complaints. But it retires a trust risk directly: the reason people abandon these migrations is fear of silent corruption, and a preview you can inspect before committing is the antidote.

Decision 4

Verifiable privacy via vendored libraries

The three third-party libraries are vendored into vendor/ and loaded from disk, so the page works with the network turned off — which is also the easiest way to verify the privacy claim.

This is the sharpest single move in the product. Everyone in this category asserts privacy. This one makes it falsifiable by the user: pull your network cable, and the tool still works, therefore nothing left your machine. That converts a marketing claim into a testable property, and retires the dominant value risk in the category without asking anyone to trust me.

Decision 5

Isolating volatile matching logic in findSidecarFor()

Google's sidecar naming is inconsistent: it truncates long names at 46 characters, moves duplicate counters around, and ships two different sidecar formats in the same export. All of that guesswork is isolated in one function, so the rules can change in one place when Google changes the format again.

This is the product thesis expressed as architecture. Part B proved the format changes and breaks tools; GPTH broke on exactly this and, being abandoned, stayed broken. Isolating the volatile logic is a bet that the environment is hostile and will change, and a decision to make the inevitable maintenance cheap. That is product judgment about the problem's dynamics, not engineering flourish.

Decision 6

Non-technical naming and up-front disclaimers

Buttons and section headings are named for non-technical users, with disclaimers and instructions placed up front to pre-empt anticipated roadblocks. Under Cagan this is the usability risk, and it is also the entire wedge against the competition. Every serious competitor is a CLI or requires installation. Naming things for humans is the product.

Decision 7

A design system with runtime-computed contrast checking

Shipped alongside the tool: tokens.css (portable raw values), style.css (components), DESIGN.md and a design/ folder documenting the reasoning, and contrast-check.html, which recomputes every contrast ratio in the system at runtime in the browser rather than asserting ratios in prose.

This is the same intellectual move as verifiable privacy, applied to accessibility: do not assert the ratio, compute it live so it cannot rot. This page is styled entirely from that token file, unchanged — which is the portability claim tested rather than stated.

Decision 8

Repo hygiene reflecting the privacy thesis

No sample Takeout data is committed and .gitignore enforces it, because sidecar JSON files carry GPS coordinates and therefore constitute personal data. The product's core belief — that this data is sensitive — is enforced even in the repo's plumbing. Also shipped: a vendored-dependency licence table, an MIT licence, and a trademark disclaimer.

Business viability, stated honestly

There is no business model, and for a portfolio artifact there does not need to be one. But if this were a real venture, viability is the weakest of the four risks. Willingness to pay clearly exists: Metadata Fixer's core app is $39 as a lifetime deal, Proper Takeout is €24.99 one-time after a free tier, and Quiver Photos charges $50 for a lifetime licence. People pay $25–50 to solve this. A free client-side tool has no capture mechanism and no funded support channel. I am not claiming a business; I am claiming product judgment.

What I got wrong, or would change

  • No hosted URL — the biggest inconsistency between thesis and artifact. A tool whose entire pitch is "no install, just open it" was distributed as a repo you had to clone. Fixed: this page and the tool are now live on GitHub Pages.
  • Licence ambiguity. jszip@3.10.1 is dual-licensed MIT or GPLv3; the licence table should state which one I am exercising, to avoid ambiguity for anyone reusing the repo.
  • HEIC will disappoint some users. JPEG-only is the right launch scope, but HEIC is now common on iPhones, and "counted and skipped" will block a meaningful subset. I would measure how often it is the blocker before building it.
  • Zero users means zero telemetry-backed claims. Every statement about user behaviour here is reasoned from third-party evidence, not my own data — which the tool deliberately does not collect. That is the correct privacy choice and a real analytics blind spot at the same time. I would not add telemetry; I would run consented usability sessions instead.

Part E

What I would do next

The honest headline: capability is not the constraint, distribution is. The tool works and embodies a clear thesis, but it has one commit, no stars and no users. Nothing else on a roadmap matters until a stranger can use it in one click and I can learn from real usage. A tool with zero users cannot support market-share claims, and this document does not make any.

I prioritise with RICE (Sean McBride, Intercom) because its Reach term forces distribution above feature-building, which is exactly the discipline needed here. Impact is scored 0.25–3, effort in person-days. These are my estimates and I flag them as such.

Under Teresa Torres's Opportunity Solution Tree, the desired outcome is not "more features" — it is "a stranger successfully repairs their library and trusts the result." The top opportunities are discovery, trust and reach problems, so the highest-value next steps attack those rather than the backlog.

Part F

What I would kill

Using the Blue Ocean ERRC grid (Kim and Mauborgne, 2005), which maps unusually well onto "what I would not build," because the strategy is to compete on axes the incumbents ignore rather than to out-feature them.

Eliminate

  • The command line. Every free incumbent competes here; it is the single biggest reason non-technical users cannot self-serve. Killing it is the wedge.
  • Installation and accounts. Both are abandonment points, and both are unnecessary for a client-side tool.
  • Server-side processing and telemetry. Eliminated on purpose — it is the privacy moat.

Reduce

  • Scope at launch: JPEG only. Reduce surface area to the fields that define the memory.
  • Configuration. No dozens of flags. Correct-by-default beats configurable.

Raise

  • Trust: verifiable privacy, preview-before-write, never touch originals — far above a category whose norm is "run this script and hope."
  • Correctness: GPS-derived timezone with DST and OffsetTime* tags, above even most paid tools' defaults.
  • Plain-language design: name everything for humans.

Create

  • A browser-based, zero-install, client-side repairer for non-technical users. No prominent free tool occupies this exact box.
  • Runtime-verifiable claims — privacy via network-off, accessibility via live contrast recomputation. "Prove it yourself" properties are a category-level novelty.

Things I would kill if proposed

  • A cloud or upload version "for convenience." It would destroy the only real moat to save a step the client-side design already removes. Kill on sight.
  • Accounts, saved history, a dashboard. Retention theatre for a tool people use once or twice. It adds a database, a privacy liability and infra cost for no user value.
  • A paid tier, now. There is willingness to pay in the category, but monetising a trust-first free tool with zero users would poison distribution before it starts.
  • Telemetry "to understand users." It contradicts the thesis and would be the first thing a sceptical user checks for. Use consented manual sessions instead.

An honesty check on positioning

The "no free browser-based client-side repairer" claim held up under research, but the free-tool landscape is not static. GPTH is effectively abandoned — last release September 2023, last commit January 2025, many unanswered open issues, broken on the new format — but an active community fork exists that does write EXIF and handle the new sidecar format, and there is a newer free GUI desktop app. Both are still installed desktop tools, not zero-install browser tools, so the positioning stands. But it should be stated as "the only free zero-install browser-based option," not "the only free option." Overclaiming here is exactly the kind of thing an interviewer will fact-check.
