Save the Dates

Every photo says today's date. That's the file's modified date, reset when you unzipped, not something Google stripped out of your pictures.

Most camera and phone photos already carry their real date inside them and are not touched. Every file with a date to be found, whether inside it, in its Live Photo partner, or in Google's record, comes out of the zip carrying that date. JPEG, HEIC, RAW and video alike. Only two things genuinely need the .json sidecar: photos that never had a date of their own, like screenshots and pictures saved out of WhatsApp, and any date or location you corrected inside Google Photos.

This one needs a computer

A phone browser could hand this page a .zip, so getting your export in is not the problem. Building the repaired copy is. That means holding whole photos in memory while the new zip is assembled, and phone browsers are given a small fraction of what a laptop gets, so a real library runs out partway through.

Starting something on your photo library that cannot finish is worse than being told to come back on a laptop, so this page says so instead of letting you find out an hour in. Open it on a desktop or laptop and everything is here.

Good to know

Give this the zip files exactly as Google sent them. Nothing needs unpacking first, and the zips themselves are only ever read, never altered. The .json files inside them are where Google put anything your photos are missing. A mix of file types is fine: everything comes back correctly dated, and only JPEGs are ever written to.

If Google gave you several zip files, choose all of them together. Google splits one library across numbered parts wherever the size limit happens to fall, so a photo can be in one part while the date for it is in another. Handing over the whole set lets them be matched up. If there is a gap in the numbers, this says which one is missing before it starts. It cannot know how many parts you were sent, so a set that stops short at the end looks complete to it. Check the highest number against Google's email.

Why do all my photos say today's date?

Two different dates get confused here. A photo file has a hidden section inside it, the EXIF data, recording when the picture was taken and where you were standing. Separately, the file itself has a "date modified" that your computer keeps, and those are not the same field.

Unzipping stamps every file it extracts with the moment it was extracted. That is what you're looking at. The modified date says today, Finder and Explorer sort by it, and twenty years of birthdays and ordinary Tuesdays collapse into one day. The EXIF inside your photos is almost always still there, untouched.

Google does not strip the EXIF out of a photo that had it. Measuring a real 65-file export: 35 of the 45 JPEGs came out with their capture date still embedded, and all 7 files named .HEIC kept both date and location. What the .json sidecars are genuinely the only copy of is narrower, and it matters:

  • Photos that never had EXIF at all: screenshots, images saved out of WhatsApp or Messenger, scans, downloads, anything shared into your library.
  • Any date, time, timezone or location you edited inside Google Photos. Those corrections were never written back into the file.

So Save the Dates does two things. Every file in your download gets its real date as the file's modified date, so the whole library lands correctly the moment you unzip, with HEIC, RAW and video included and not a byte inside them touched. And for the JPEGs that are genuinely missing something, it fills that in from the sidecar. It shows you which is which, one row per file, before it writes anything.

Why some dates are marked "Google's record"

When a photo has no date inside it, Google's sidecar still offers one. It is tempting to treat that as the recovered capture date. It usually isn't. For anything uploaded or shared into your library rather than shot on the device, that timestamp is when Google first saw the photo.

The giveaway is clustering. In a real export, ten date-less photos had sidecar timestamps spanning sixteen seconds in total. Ten photos taken across different years do not arrive sixteen seconds apart. That is one upload, recorded once.

Save the Dates labels every date with where it came from, flags groups that look like an upload batch, and leaves those dates out of your photos unless you explicitly ask for them. They still get the right date on the outside of the file, so your library sorts sensibly. What's written inside stays honest.

What is Google Takeout, and why would I use it?

Google Takeout is Google's official tool for downloading a copy of your own data: photos, email, contacts, documents. It's free, and it's at takeout.google.com.

Most people end up there for one of these reasons:

  • You stopped paying for Google One and want your photos before the storage runs out
  • You're switching to Apple Photos, iCloud, Immich, Ente, or just a hard drive
  • You want a backup that doesn't depend on one company's account staying open
  • You're consolidating a family archive from several accounts into one place

Takeout is the only way to get your full-resolution originals out of Google Photos in bulk. Downloading them by hand from the website caps out at a few at a time.

How to export just your photos

Takeout defaults to exporting everything in your Google account: your email, your Drive, your search history, dozens of services. You almost certainly don't want that. Here's how to get photos only.

  1. Go to takeout.google.com and check your account

    Look at the profile picture in the top right. Takeout exports whichever account you're signed into. If you have more than one, make sure it's the right one.

  2. Click "Deselect all"

    It's at the top of the long list of services. This is the single most important click on the page. Everything is checked by default.

  3. Scroll down and check "Google Photos" only

    The list is alphabetical and long. Google Photos is roughly two-thirds of the way down. Check that one box and nothing else.

  4. Leave the format settings alone

    You'll see "Multiple formats" and "All photo albums included." The defaults are correct. Don't change them. If you want to try this tool out first, click "All photo albums included" and pick one small album instead. A 30-photo export finishes in minutes; a full library can take days.

  5. Click "Next step" at the very bottom
  6. Choose your delivery settings

    Destination: "Send download link via email", which is the simplest option. Frequency: "Export once." File type: .zip. Size: 2 GB. Google splits your library across multiple files at whatever size you pick. Smaller files mean more of them, but each one downloads faster and is less likely to fail partway through, and a failed download has to be started again. If you're not sure, pick 2 GB.

  7. Click "Create export"
  8. Wait for the email

    A small album can be ready in minutes. A few thousand photos usually takes a few hours. A very large library can take one to two days. Google emails you when it's done.

  9. Download every single file

    If Google gives you seven files, download all seven. A photo's date can live in one file while the photo lives in another, so a partial set gives you a partial result. The download links expire after about a week. If you miss the window, you have to start the export over. If one of them is a bare .mp4 rather than a .zip, that is normal: Google does that when a single video is larger than the size you picked. Keep it with the others.

  10. That's it. Bring them here as they are

    Don't unzip anything. Drag all the files onto this page together, or use Choose zip files and select the whole set. Save the Dates reads inside the zips without unpacking them, which is also why it doesn't need room on your disk for a second copy of your library. If there is a gap in the numbers it says so before it starts, though it cannot tell that the last part is missing, so check the highest number against what Google sent.

Don't delete the .json files. They look like junk. They are not junk, because they are where Google put anything your photos don't already carry, and this tool reads them.

How to save the dates back to your Google Photos export
  1. Download every file Google sent you. Leave them zipped
  2. Click "Choose zip files" and select all of them at once, or drag them onto the page
  3. Check what it found. Every file is listed with what will happen to it, before anything is written
  4. Click "Download fixed photos" and save the zip that comes back

Your Google Takeout folder is never modified. Save the Dates hands you a new copy with the dates restored.

Your photos never leave your computer

There is no server. There is no upload. Everything happens inside your browser on your own machine, the same way a calculator app works offline.

You can verify this yourself: disconnect from the internet after the page loads. The tool keeps working.

No account. No sign-up. No email address. No tracking. No ads. The code is open source and you can read all of it.

What this doesn't do (yet)
  • Only JPEGs are ever written to. HEIC, RAW and video go into your download with the right date on them, but nothing inside them is changed. In testing they didn't need it: all 7 files named .HEIC in a real export came out of Takeout with their date and location already intact. There is nothing there to repair, so this tool doesn't pretend to.
  • Dates that exist only in Google's sidecar are not written into your photos unless you tick the box asking for it. They are frequently the moment the photo entered your library, not when it was taken.
  • Opening a zip needs a browser from 2023 or later, because it uses the decompressor built into the browser rather than shipping one. On anything older, unzip the export yourself and use Choose folder, which still works exactly as it did.
  • Photos with no location get UTC. Google stores every capture time in UTC, and photo files store plain local time with no timezone attached. When a photo has GPS in its .json file, Save the Dates works out the timezone of the place it was taken and converts to the clock time there, daylight saving included. When it has no GPS there is nothing to work that out from, so the time is written in UTC exactly as Google stored it, which can read a few hours off.
  • Very large libraries may struggle. Everything runs in your browser's memory. If you have tens of thousands of photos, do it in batches, one year at a time.

If you need your album structure rebuilt, Live Photos paired back up, or a 200 GB library handled in one pass, use GooglePhotosTakeoutHelper Neo. It's free, actively maintained, and handles all of it. It's a desktop program you install; this is the browser option for the common case.

Questions
Why bother putting the dates back at all?
Because almost everything you do with photos afterwards depends on them. With the dates back, your library sorts into the order things actually happened, "on this day" memories start working again, and searching for a year or a place finds something. Apple Photos, iCloud, Immich and Ente all build their timelines from this data. Without it, twenty years of photos is one enormous pile stamped with the day you clicked download. Everything is there, and you can't find any of it.
Is Save the Dates really free?
Yes. No cap, no trial, no paid tier, no ads. It's a portfolio project, not a business.
Do I need to install anything?
No. Save the Dates is a web page. Google Takeout is a website too, so neither step needs an install.
Does Save the Dates work on my phone?
No. A phone browser could hand this page a .zip, so getting your export in is not what stops it. Building the repaired copy is: that holds whole photos in memory while the new zip is assembled, and a phone browser is allowed a small fraction of a laptop's room for it, so a real library runs out partway through. The page says so rather than letting you start something that cannot finish. Use a desktop or laptop.
Will Save the Dates change my original photos?
No. Save the Dates builds a new copy and hands it to you as a zip download. The zips or folder you chose are only ever read, never written to.
My export is a mix of JPEGs, HEICs and videos. Do I have to sort them first?
No. Hand over the whole export, zips and all, or the whole Takeout folder if you unpacked it. Everything comes back in the download with the correct date on it. Only JPEGs that are missing something get written to; the HEICs, RAW files and videos are copied through byte for byte, and the screen tells you which is which.
Can I delete Google's .json files afterward?
Once you've confirmed the dates look right in the fixed copies, yes. They are Google Takeout's metadata files, not part of your photos.
Why do some files say "No date anywhere"?
Because there was no date inside the file and no usable one in a sidecar either. Often the sidecar simply couldn't be matched: Google Takeout's file naming is inconsistent, because it truncates long filenames and renames duplicates in ways that don't always line up with the photo they belong to. Those files are still in your download, keeping the date they already had. The preview report has a "sidecar filename used" column so you can see which ones found no match.
What about photos I edited in Google Photos?
Google Takeout exports those with "-edited" in the filename and no .json file of their own. Save the Dates gives them the date from the original photo.
