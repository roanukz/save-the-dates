Why do all my photos say today's date?

Every photo file has a hidden section inside it — the EXIF data — that stores when the picture was taken and where you were standing. It's how your phone builds a timeline and how "photos from Paris" search works.

When Google exports your library, it doesn't leave that information inside the photos. It pulls the date and location out and writes them into separate .json files sitting next to each image.

The photos themselves come out blank. Every app you open them in — Apple Photos, Lightroom, Windows Explorer, your Mac's Finder — asks the file when it was taken, gets no answer, and falls back to the day you downloaded it.

The result: twenty years of birthdays, holidays and ordinary Tuesdays all collapse into a single day. The information isn't lost. It's just sitting in the wrong file.

Save the Dates reads those .json files and writes the dates and coordinates back inside your photos, where every other app can find them.

What is Google Takeout, and why would I use it?

Google Takeout is Google's official tool for downloading a copy of your own data — photos, email, contacts, documents. It's free, and it's at takeout.google.com.

Most people end up there for one of these reasons:

  • You stopped paying for Google One and want your photos before the storage runs out
  • You're switching to Apple Photos, iCloud, Immich, Ente, or just a hard drive
  • You want a backup that doesn't depend on one company's account staying open
  • You're consolidating a family archive from several accounts into one place

Takeout is the only way to get your full-resolution originals out of Google Photos in bulk. Downloading them by hand from the website caps out at a few at a time.

How to export just your photos

Takeout defaults to exporting everything in your Google account — your email, your Drive, your search history, dozens of services. You almost certainly don't want that. Here's how to get photos only.

  1. Go to takeout.google.com and check your account

    Look at the profile picture in the top right. Takeout exports whichever account you're signed into. If you have more than one, make sure it's the right one.

  2. Click "Deselect all"

    It's at the top of the long list of services. This is the single most important click on the page. Everything is checked by default.

  3. Scroll down and check "Google Photos" only

    The list is alphabetical and long. Google Photos is roughly two-thirds of the way down. Check that one box and nothing else.

  4. Leave the format settings alone

    You'll see "Multiple formats" and "All photo albums included." The defaults are correct. Don't change them. If you want to try this tool out first, click "All photo albums included" and pick one small album instead. A 30-photo export finishes in minutes; a full library can take days.

  5. Click "Next step" at the very bottom
  6. Choose your delivery settings

    Destination: "Send download link via email" — this is the simplest option. Frequency: "Export once." File type: .zip. Size: 2 GB. Google splits your library across multiple files at whatever size you pick. Smaller files mean more of them, but each one downloads and unzips faster and is less likely to fail partway through. If you're not sure, pick 2 GB.

  7. Click "Create export"
  8. Wait for the email

    A small album can be ready in minutes. A few thousand photos usually takes a few hours. A very large library can take one to two days. Google emails you when it's done.

  9. Download every single file before you unzip anything

    If Google gives you seven files, download all seven first. A photo's date can live in one file while the photo lives in another, and unzipping a partial set gives you a partial result. The download links expire after about a week. If you miss the window, you have to start the export over.

  10. Unzip them all into one folder

    Double-click each .zip file. Put all the contents in the same place. You'll end up with a folder called Takeout containing a folder called Google Photos.

Don't delete the .json files. They look like junk. They are not junk — they are where Google put your dates and locations, and this tool needs them.

How to save the dates back to your Google Photos export
  1. Unzip your Google Takeout download — you need the folder, not the zip
  2. Click "Choose folder" and pick that unzipped Takeout folder
  3. Check what it found — every file is listed with what will happen to it, before anything is written
  4. Click "Download fixed photos" and save the zip that comes back

Your Google Takeout folder is never modified. Save the Dates hands you a new copy with the dates restored.

Your photos never leave your computer

There is no server. There is no upload. Everything happens inside your browser on your own machine — the same way a calculator app works offline.

You can verify this yourself: disconnect from the internet after the page loads. The tool keeps working.

No account. No sign-up. No email address. No tracking. No ads. The code is open source and you can read all of it.

What this doesn't do (yet)
  • JPEG only. HEIC, PNG and video aren't handled yet — but you don't have to separate them out. Point Save the Dates at the whole folder: it fixes the JPEGs, lists everything else so you can see what was left, and doesn't touch those files.
  • You have to unzip first. It reads folders, not .zip files.
  • Photos with no location get UTC. Google stores every capture time in UTC, and photo files store plain local time with no timezone attached. When a photo has GPS in its .json file, Save the Dates works out the timezone of the place it was taken and converts to the clock time there, daylight saving included. When it has no GPS there is nothing to work that out from, so the time is written in UTC exactly as Google stored it — which can read a few hours off.
  • Very large libraries may struggle. Everything runs in your browser's memory. If you have tens of thousands of photos, do it in batches — one year at a time.

If you need HEIC, video, or a 200 GB library handled in one pass, there are paid desktop apps that do it well. This is the free option for the common case.

Questions
Why bother putting the dates back at all?
Because almost everything you do with photos afterwards depends on them. With the dates back, your library sorts into the order things actually happened, "on this day" memories start working again, and searching for a year or a place finds something. Apple Photos, iCloud, Immich and Ente all build their timelines from this data. Without it, twenty years of photos is one enormous pile stamped with the day you clicked download — everything is there, and you can't find any of it.
Is Save the Dates really free?
Yes. No cap, no trial, no paid tier, no ads. It's a portfolio project, not a business.
Do I need to install anything?
No. Save the Dates is a web page. Google Takeout is a website too, so neither step needs an install.
Does Save the Dates work on my phone?
Not well. You need to pick a folder, which phone browsers don't really do. Use a computer.
Will Save the Dates change my original photos?
No. Save the Dates builds a new copy and hands it to you as a zip download. The Google Takeout folder you picked is only read, never written to.
My export is a mix of JPEGs, HEICs and videos. Do I have to sort them first?
No — point Save the Dates at the whole Google Takeout folder. It fixes the JPEGs, and every other file is listed on screen and in the report as "can't be fixed" so you can see exactly what was left out. Those files aren't moved, changed or deleted; they simply aren't in the download.
Can I delete Google's .json files afterward?
Once you've confirmed the dates look right in the fixed copies, yes. They are Google Takeout's metadata files, not part of your photos.
Why does Save the Dates say some photos are unmatched?
Google Takeout's file naming is inconsistent — it truncates long filenames and renames duplicates in ways that don't always line up with the photo they belong to. Save the Dates lists every unmatched photo so you can see exactly which ones need attention, rather than silently skipping them.
What about photos I edited in Google Photos?
Google Takeout exports those with "-edited" in the filename and no .json file of their own. Save the Dates gives them the date from the original photo.
