The short version

Summary

  • The problem is real, documented and durable. Unzipping a Takeout export resets every file's modified date, and for photos that never carried EXIF, or whose dates were edited inside Google Photos, the JSON sidecar is the only copy. Google's late-2024 rename to .supplemental-metadata.json broke the leading free tool. The claim that this position was empty is no longer true, and I have retracted it: a free browser-based client-side repairer now exists. What survives is narrower, dated and checkable, and it is set out in part 7.
  • Measurement set the scope, not intuition. The category's shared explanation is that Takeout strips capture dates out of photos. One afternoon with ExifTool over a real 65-file export showed it does not, which cut a 10-day roadmap item and pointed the product at what is actually broken. It is the highest-leverage work on the project, and it cost a day.
  • The product judgment is in the costly decisions, not the code. GPS-derived timezone (a documented reversal), refusing by default to write Google's upload timestamp into a field that reads as a capture time, two deliberately-hard-to-confuse CSV reports, and privacy you can verify rather than take on trust.
  • The binding constraint is distribution, not capability. With one commit and no users, this cannot support market-share claims, and this teardown does not make any. The top three roadmap items are all distribution, not features. That ordering is the point.

How this was built

This tool was built with AI assistance (Claude Code), against decisions I made and own. I chose the client-side architecture to make privacy verifiable, reversed the original timezone plan in favour of the harder GPS-derived approach because travel photos are the memories people most care about, and isolated Google's volatile matching rules in one function because the export format keeps breaking other tools. I also measured a live export with ExifTool rather than taking the category's explanation on trust, which is what cut HEIC support and pointed the scope at the file dates. The AI wrote code against those decisions; the decisions are mine.

Part 1

The problem

When someone leaves Google Photos, they use Google Takeout to export their library, and every photo lands stamped with today's date. The cause is not the one it looks like. Unzipping sets each file's modified date to the moment it was extracted, and Finder, Explorer and a good many import tools sort by that field, so a twenty-year library collapses into a single day. The EXIF inside most of those photos is untouched. Where the export genuinely does lose the when and the where is narrower: photos that never carried EXIF at all, and any date, time or location corrected inside Google Photos. Both of those live only in Google's JSON sidecars, and most photo apps, among them Apple Photos, Lightroom, Immich and a NAS, read EXIF rather than sidecars.

What the export actually contained

Every guide and forum thread on this problem repeats the same explanation: Takeout strips your capture dates out into the JSON sidecars. It is worth checking, because the whole shape of a tool follows from it. I ran ExifTool over a real 65-file export spanning 2013 to 2024.

It does not strip them. 35 of the 45 JPEGs still carried DateTimeOriginal, and all 7 files named .HEIC came out with date and GPS fully intact. The sidecar even carries a geoDataExif block duplicating coordinates already in the file: Google copies, it does not move. What resets for every single file is the file system's modified date, because unzipping stamps each extracted file with the extraction time. Different field, and the one people are actually looking at.

Two further findings shaped the build. Only 3 of those 7 .HEIC files were genuinely HEIC-encoded; Google had converted the other 4 to JPEG while leaving the extension in place, so the tool identifies formats by their bytes and never by filename. And the 10 files that genuinely had no embedded date carried sidecar timestamps spanning 16 seconds in total, across photos taken years apart, which is an upload batch rather than ten capture times. Decisions 2 and 3 follow directly from those numbers.

Framed in Jobs To Be Done (Clayton Christensen; the hire/fire formulation and the milkshake study from Competing Against Luck, 2016), the user is not hiring a "metadata tool." The functional job is: when I move my photo library off Google, help me keep my memories in the right time and place so my new app shows my life in order.

The emotional and social dimensions are what give the job its weight. These are birthdays, trips, and a child growing up, and a scrambled timeline feels like losing the memory itself. The firing event is concrete and dated: a user runs an export, imports it, sees everything stamped with today's date, and starts searching for a fix.

Freshness check, late 2025 into 2026

The problem is still live and, if anything, worse. The late-2024 rename is still rolling out inconsistently, so a single export can contain the old format, the new format, or both. Google also truncates filenames at 46 characters, producing a slot machine of truncated sidecar names: .supplemental-metadat.json, .supple.json, .s.json. Guides updated in March and April 2026 still describe the wrong-date problem as current. There is no evidence Google has fixed the export behaviour.

This is a durable, recurring, independently verified problem, and the format keeps changing in ways that break tools. That last fact is central to the product thesis, so see decision 6, which is where the guesswork about Google's naming is deliberately kept in one place.

Part 3

Why the incumbent does not serve it

Google could re-embed metadata into files at export time. It does not. The honest framing is incentive analysis, not accusation, and I am interpreting motive here, so I flag it as inference rather than proven intent.

1. Storage economics

Google does not reassemble a faithful original at export. Edits made inside Google Photos are held in Google's own metadata store and written out as JSON rather than merged back into the file, and the export makes no attempt to carry file timestamps through the zip. Doing either is extra engineering work on data Google deliberately holds separately. There is no upside for Google.

A caveat on the sourcing: the community threads on this problem often go further and claim Google strips metadata out of the files themselves. Measurement says otherwise, so I am not resting the argument on that. The narrower version is enough.

2. Retention friction

A software engineer who documented his own 263 GB extraction argued the friction is a strategy: every one of these friction points exists because Google calculated that most people will give up and keep paying for Google One. Friction on the way out protects subscriptions.

The load-bearing claim is the weaker one

There is no investigative-journalism proof of deliberate malice, and one cited engineer conceded it is unlikely there was any ill intent. So the claim I am actually making is smaller and more defensible: Google has no incentive to fix it, therefore the gap persists. That is enough to explain an unsolved problem this old.

This is the structural-moat point. The gap is not a Google oversight that Google will close next quarter and crush a small tool. It is a stable consequence of Google's cost structure and retention incentives. The opportunity is durable precisely because the incumbent is structurally uninterested.

Part 4

What I built

Save the Dates is a single-page, 100% client-side browser tool. You open tool.html; that is the entire install. No server, no build step, no package manager, no accounts, no database, no analytics, no telemetry, and no network calls at runtime. It restores each photo's real capture date as the file's modified date, and reads Takeout's JSON sidecars to fill in the EXIF of the photos that are genuinely missing a date or a location. It hands back a new copy as a download and never modifies originals.

I use Marty Cagan's four product risks (Inspired, SVPG: value, usability, feasibility, viability) as the spine, because each interesting decision retires a specific risk.

Decision 1

GPS-derived timezone: a documented reversal

The original plan recommended the simpler approach: convert the UTC timestamp to the browser's local timezone, disclose it in the UI, and file GPS-derived inference as a future enhancement. I reversed this and built the harder, more correct version, which derives the timezone from the photo's GPS position using tz-lookup, not from the machine running the tool. A photo taken at 3pm in London reads 3pm whether the tool runs at home or abroad. Daylight saving is resolved for the actual date via Intl. Photos with no GPS fall back to UTC, exactly as Google stored it. The tool also writes the OffsetTime* tags so apps that understand them can recover the true instant.

  • CostAn extra vendored dependency and added complexity.
  • BoughtCorrect times for travel photos, which are disproportionately the memories people care about and the emotional core of the job.
  • WhyThe browser-local approach fails silently and worst precisely for the highest-value photos, and the failure stays invisible until much later.

Under Cagan, this is a deliberate acceptance of higher feasibility cost to retire a value risk: a costly commitment to correctness against the easier path, with the tradeoff stated rather than hidden.

Scoped by decision 2. This machinery only applies to dates taken from the sidecar. A date already inside a photo is used exactly as the camera recorded it, unconverted. Because most photos keep their own date, and decision 3 holds sidecar-only dates back by default, it runs on a narrow slice of a library rather than all of it. Still right for that slice, and a fair illustration of how much cheaper it is to measure first than to build first.

Decision 2

Letting the measurement set the scope, and cut a roadmap item

The numbers in part 1 pointed at a different product from the one the category assumes. If most photos already carry their date, then re-writing their EXIF is not the job. What is broken for every file, of every format, is the date on the outside of it.

So the tool inverts the usual scope. Every file leaves in the download carrying its real date, whether JPEG, HEIC, RAW or video, without a byte inside it being touched, and only JPEGs genuinely missing something are written to. On the measured export that means it writes nothing at all by default; it simply hands back 65 correctly dated files.

The same numbers cut HEIC support, which had been scoped at 10+ days. It existed to solve a problem nobody had verified, and the files it targeted turned out to need nothing. Stated honestly: only 3 of the 7 were genuinely HEIC-encoded, so the sample behind the cut is small, and I would re-measure on a larger library before treating it as settled.

  • CostA day spent measuring rather than building, and a smaller headline claim than the category's.
  • BoughtA 10-day roadmap item cut, and a scope that matches what is actually broken.
  • WhyAn unverified premise propagates into architecture, copy and roadmap at once. Checking it is the cheapest work available.

Under Kano, safe passthrough of files you do not process is a must-be. The measurement showed the must-be was the product: correct file dates on everything is what most users actually needed, and it was nearly free.

Decision 3

Refusing to write a guess into a field that looks like a fact

For the 10 photos in my export with no embedded date, Google's sidecar still offered one, and it is tempting to write it in and call the date recovered. Those 10 timestamps spanned 16 seconds in total, across photos taken years apart. It was one upload, recorded once. It was never a capture time.

The tool now labels the source of every date on screen, showing "from the photo" against "Google's record", detects the upload-batch pattern and says so in plain language, and leaves those dates out of the file unless you explicitly tick a box. Those files still get the right date on the outside, so the library sorts correctly; what is written inside stays honest.

  • CostThe default now does visibly less, and the results screen argues against the tool's own pitch.
  • BoughtAn irreversible, invisible error avoided: once a guess is in DateTimeOriginal, nothing downstream can tell it from a real one.
  • WhySilent, permanent, plausible-looking wrongness is the worst failure mode in this category, and the one users cannot detect.

Under Cagan this retires a value risk the category ignores: every competitor writes the sidecar timestamp in without comment. Data provenance as a visible, per-row feature is the most defensible thing this product does, and I have not found another tool that makes the distinction at all.

Decision 4

Two reports: preview versus results

A PREVIEW report of what will be written, and a RESULTS report of what was. They are deliberately hard to confuse, since one describes photos nothing has happened to yet.

Under Kano this is a delighter. No competitor offers it, most users would not have asked for it, and its absence would generate no complaints. But it retires a trust risk directly: the reason people abandon these migrations is fear of silent corruption, and a preview you can inspect before committing is the antidote.

Decision 5

Verifiable privacy via vendored libraries

The three third-party libraries are vendored into vendor/ and loaded from disk, so the page works with the network turned off, which is also the easiest way to verify the privacy claim.

This is the sharpest single move in the product. Everyone in this category asserts privacy. This one makes it falsifiable by the user: pull your network cable, and the tool still works, therefore nothing left your machine. That converts a marketing claim into a testable property, and retires the dominant value risk in the category without asking anyone to trust me.

Decision 6

Isolating volatile matching logic in findSidecarFor()

Google's sidecar naming is inconsistent: it truncates long names at 46 characters, moves duplicate counters around, and ships two different sidecar formats in the same export. All of that guesswork is isolated in one function, so the rules can change in one place when Google changes the format again.

This is the product thesis expressed as architecture. Part 2 proved the format changes and breaks tools; GPTH broke on exactly this and, being abandoned, stayed broken. Isolating the volatile logic is a bet that the environment is hostile and will change, and a decision to make the inevitable maintenance cheap. That is product judgment about the problem's dynamics, not engineering flourish.

Decision 7

Non-technical naming and up-front disclaimers

Buttons and section headings are named for non-technical users, with disclaimers and instructions placed up front to pre-empt anticipated roadblocks. Under Cagan this is the usability risk, and it is also the entire wedge against the competition. Every serious competitor is a CLI or requires installation. Naming things for humans is the product.

Decision 8

A design system with runtime-computed contrast checking

Shipped alongside the tool: tokens.css (portable raw values), style.css (components), DESIGN.md and a design/ folder documenting the reasoning, and contrast-check.html, which recomputes every contrast ratio in the system at runtime in the browser rather than asserting ratios in prose.

This is the same intellectual move as verifiable privacy, applied to accessibility: do not assert the ratio, compute it live so it cannot rot. This page is styled entirely from that token file, unchanged, which is the portability claim tested rather than stated.

Decision 9

Repo hygiene reflecting the privacy thesis

No sample Takeout data is committed and .gitignore enforces it, because sidecar JSON files carry GPS coordinates and therefore constitute personal data. The product's core belief, that this data is sensitive, is enforced even in the repo's plumbing. Also shipped: a vendored-dependency licence table, an MIT licence, and a trademark disclaimer.

Business viability, stated honestly

There is no business model, and for a portfolio artifact there does not need to be one. But if this were a real venture, viability is the weakest of the four risks. Willingness to pay clearly exists: Metadata Fixer's core app is $39 as a lifetime deal, Proper Takeout is €24.99 one-time after a free tier, and Quiver Photos charges $50 for a lifetime licence. People pay between $25 and $50 to solve this. A free client-side tool has no capture mechanism and no funded support channel. I am not claiming a business; I am claiming product judgment.

What I got wrong, or would change

  • I measured too late in the process. The ExifTool run that set the scope came after most of the build, not before it. An hour on day one would have scoped decision 1 differently and kept HEIC support off the roadmap entirely. Verifying the premise is the cheapest work available on a project like this, and I did it in the wrong order. The habit I took from it: make the product measure the thing rather than assert it, which is why the preview table now reports per file whether it needed the tool at all. And I did it again in v2. Two claims about how Google packs an archive went into a code comment as reasoning before either was checked, and both were wrong. The difference is that this time the checking happened before it shipped rather than after, and part 6 publishes both retractions. That is an improvement in when, not yet in habit.
  • The obvious implementation would have written a guess into a field that reads as a fact. Every tool in this category takes Google's photoTakenTime for a date-less photo and writes it into DateTimeOriginal. It is the natural thing to build, and on my export it would have stamped 10 photos taken years apart with timestamps 16 seconds apart, destroying the distinction between a known capture time and a guess, permanently and invisibly. Catching it needed the data, not more thought. It is the strongest argument I have for measuring before designing.
  • No hosted URL. This was the biggest inconsistency between thesis and artifact. A tool whose entire pitch is "no install, just open it" was distributed as a repo you had to clone. Fixed: this page and the tool are now live on GitHub Pages.
  • Licence ambiguity. jszip@3.10.1 is dual-licensed MIT or GPLv3; the licence table should state which one I am exercising, to avoid ambiguity for anyone reusing the repo.
  • The HEIC sample is thin. Only 3 files in the export were genuinely HEIC-encoded, and I cut a 10-day roadmap item partly on that. It is the right call on the evidence I have, but three files is not a study, and I would re-measure across a few libraries before treating it as settled. Partly answered, from someone else's evidence rather than mine: the maintained fork of the leading desktop tool documents that Google's "Storage Saver" compresses images to JPEG while keeping the original filename extension, which is exactly the pattern I found and could not generalise from. A rival codebase independently describing the same behaviour is better evidence than three more of my own files, and it is what "sniff the bytes, never the extension" was built for. The sample is still thin; the decision it supported is no longer resting on it alone.
  • Zero users means zero telemetry-backed claims. Every statement about user behaviour here is reasoned from third-party evidence, not my own data, which the tool deliberately does not collect. That is the correct privacy choice and a real analytics blind spot at the same time. I would not add telemetry; I would run consented usability sessions instead.

Part 5

The honest headline: capability is not the constraint, distribution is. That is still the finding, and part 6 is what happened when the top buildable row was built anyway: it removed two onboarding steps and changed nothing about the fact that nobody has arrived yet. The tool works and embodies a clear thesis, but it has no stars and no users. Nothing else on a roadmap matters until a stranger can use it in one click and I can learn from real usage. Part 6

What I shipped next

Part 5 put the top three next steps in distribution and scored one buildable feature above the rest: take the Takeout zip as it arrives, so nobody has to unpack anything first. The two distribution rows shipped, this page is the evidence of one of them, and that made the zip row the live one. This part is what happened when I built it, including the two things I had assumed and measured only afterwards.

What it does now

You hand it the .zip files exactly as Google sent them, all of them at once, and it reads inside them without unpacking anything. The onboarding lost two steps, and they were the two people got wrong: download every file before you unzip anything, and unzip them all into one folder. Those existed because Google splits a library wherever the size limit falls, so a photo can sit in one part while the JSON holding its date sits in another, and both halves had to be put in one place by hand before the tool could match them. It now does that joining up itself, from the paths inside the parts.

Three things came with it that were not in the original row. It notices when a numbered part is missing and asks before reading anything, because the point of noticing is to save the run, not to explain it afterwards. It takes the loose media files Google ships beside the zips when one video is bigger than the split size. And it never needs room on your disk for a second copy of your library, which for the engineer quoted in part 3, who documented a 263 GB extraction, is a larger win than the clicks.

What I assumed, and what two real exports showed

The measurement in part 1 is the thing this project is proudest of, and I did it late. So this time I wrote the argument down first and then checked it against two real Takeout exports, 91 and 121 entries, on 18 August 2026. Both halves of the argument were wrong.

Decision 10

Writing a zip reader rather than using the one already in the project

JSZip was already vendored, already loaded, and already writing the output, so reading with it would have been free. It cannot do this job. It reads an archive by pulling all of it into memory, and a Takeout part is commonly 2 GB and can be 50 GB. Worse, and the part I checked rather than assumed: it reads the index offset by shifting bytes, which JavaScript does in 32 bits and signed. I ran the shipped function on my own 2.6 GB archive's real offset of 2,621,440,000 and it returns minus 1,673,527,296, so it cannot find the index at all. That is not a large-archive limitation. It is every archive over 2 GB, which is most of them.

So the reader is a new file of about 360 lines of code and 250 of comments explaining a format most people never look at, which does what an unzip program does: read the small index at the end, then fetch one entry at a time with Blob.slice, inflating with DecompressionStream, which is built into the browser. No fourth library. The cost of opening an archive is set by the size of its index, not the size of the archive, so a 50 GB export opens like a small one. Measured on the same machine: a 2.6 GB archive indexed while holding 35 MB, and scanning every file in a real 246 MB export for its metadata never rose more than about 15 MB above idle. Timings vary with what the disk has cached and are not worth quoting; the memory does not, and it is the number that decides whether the tab survives.

  • CostFour hundred lines of format parsing I now own, in a project whose whole pitch is that it is small, plus a browser floor of Safari 16.4 and Firefox 113.
  • BoughtReading a 50 GB export without ever holding it, no new dependency, and no version of this that dies on the archive sizes Google actually offers.
  • WhyThe alternative was not "use JSZip", it was "use JSZip and quietly fail on most real exports". A dependency that cannot do the job is not cheaper than code that can.

The floor is the honest cost and it is stated in the tool rather than discovered at runtime. Anyone older unpacks the export themselves and uses the folder route, which still works exactly as it did.

Decision 11

Refusing a zip rather than half-reading one

Reading an archive introduces a failure this tool did not previously have, and it is the worst shape available to it: a zip that half-reads produces a scan that looks completely healthy while describing part of a library. Everything about this product argues that a partial set gives a partial result, so success reported over a truncated library would be the tool committing its own signature bug.

So three checks refuse rather than continue. The archive's declared file count is compared against the number actually read, because a malformed entry makes the walk lose its place and stop early with nothing to show for it. Every whole file is checked against the checksum stored beside it, because these are multi-gigabyte downloads over home connections and a damaged photo copied into the output and presented as repaired is worse than one refused. And a large-archive marker with no large-archive index throws instead of returning the placeholder, which previously produced "that zip has nothing in it" and blamed the wrong thing.

  • CostA checksum pass over every file, and a class of archive that other tools would open and this one will not.
  • BoughtNo path where a damaged or incomplete export produces a confident, wrong, complete-looking result.
  • WhyRefusing is recoverable, because Google will send the file again. Deleting your originals against a silently partial copy is not.

Writing a guarantee is not the same as having one. A review pass before this shipped found that the guarantee above had a hole straight through it: entries under 4 MB were being kept in memory after their first read, the copy was stored before the checksum ran, and a later read returned that copy without checking anything. So a damaged photo could fail verification once and be served as good every time after. The same cache also quietly retained most of the library, which is the precise opposite of the flat memory this file was built for. It bought nothing, because nothing is read in full twice in one run. It is gone, and the test page now fails if anything like it returns.

The habit that catches this is not more care while writing. It is having someone else try to break the thing after it looks finished. Both of the wrong claims above and this hole were found that way, and none of them would have been found by testing, because the code did exactly what it was written to do.

Decision 12

The packing moment is not a capture time, and it is not written anywhere

This is decision 3 all over again, reached from the other end. A file with no date inside it and no sidecar has one date left: the one stored beside it in the zip. It is tempting, because it is real, specific and much better than today. It is also the moment Google packed the archive, which the measurement above shows plainly. A hundred and twenty one entries spanning years of photographs do not share five timestamps sixty two seconds apart.

So it is treated as no date at all. Those files are counted under "no date anywhere", nothing is written inside them, and the results screen says in words that the only date left is when Google packed the export and that this is not when the photo was taken. It is still used for the date on the outside of the copy, because something has to go there and the packing moment beats the moment you happened to run this, and that use is disclosed too.

Scoped by decision 3. The rule was already written: a guess never goes into a field that reads as a fact. What was new was a second source that looks like a fact and is not.

What the estimate got wrong

The row in part 5 said three days at 70% confidence. The number is roughly right and the reasoning behind it was not, which is the more useful thing to record.

The 70% was feasibility risk: whether a browser could read a multi-gigabyte zip at all. That was the wrong risk to price. It was settled in an afternoon and never came back. What the estimate did not contain was any allowance for the two false claims above, for the checks in decision 11, or for the copy sweep, and that is where the work actually went. Reading the zip was the small part. Being able to say honestly what had been read was the rest of it.

The estimate also under-scoped in a way that flattered it: it said "drag-and-drop the whole Takeout zip", and a set of numbered parts with a gap in it is not one zip. The missing-part question is the single most useful thing in this release and it was not in the row at all.

What I have not proved

The two exports are one library, measured twice. They are the same account three days apart, 91 and 121 entries. That is enough to refute two claims I had made about Google, because one counterexample refutes, but it is nowhere near enough to describe what Google does generally. Both are 2 GB splits, so neither contains a large-format index at all.

So the 10 GB and 50 GB splits are tested only against archives built to look like them. The test page constructs one rather than downloading fifty gigabytes, and that is exactly the thin-sample problem part 4 confesses about HEIC, in a new place. It is stated here rather than discovered by someone else.

And there are still no users. Nothing here is evidence that anyone wanted it. It is evidence that the thing the roadmap named is now built and that its claims can be checked, which is a different and smaller claim.

How this can be checked without trusting me

Every number in this part came from running something, and the runnable parts are in the repository. test-fixtures/zip-tests.html opens in a browser with no install and runs 109 checks against archives it builds while you watch, including a deliberately damaged one, an archive that lies about how many files it holds, and an export split across parts. That is the same "prove it yourself" property as the network-off privacy check and the live contrast recomputation, applied to the one claim a reader has most reason to doubt: that a file read out of a zip is the same file.

Part 7

What I would cut

Using the Blue Ocean ERRC grid (Kim and Mauborgne, 2005), which maps unusually well onto "what I would not build," because the strategy is to compete on axes the incumbents ignore rather than to out-feature them.

Eliminate

  • The command line. Every free incumbent competes here; it is the single biggest reason non-technical users cannot self-serve. Cutting it is the wedge.
  • Installation and accounts. Both are abandonment points, and both are unnecessary for a client-side tool.
  • Server-side processing and telemetry. Eliminated on purpose, because it is the privacy moat.

Reduce

  • What gets written. Only JPEGs, only where something is genuinely missing. Measurement showed the rest needed nothing.
  • Configuration. Two checkboxes, not dozens of flags. Correct-by-default beats configurable.

Raise

  • Trust: verifiable privacy, preview-before-write, never touch originals, all far above a category whose norm is "run this script and hope."
  • Correctness: GPS-derived timezone with DST and OffsetTime* tags, above even most paid tools' defaults.
  • Honesty about the data: every date labelled with where it came from, upload batches called out, and a guess never written into a field that reads as a fact.
  • Plain-language design: name everything for humans.

Create

  • A browser-based, zero-install, client-side repairer for non-technical users. One other free tool now occupies this box, and the four things that still separate them are set out below.
  • Runtime-verifiable claims, meaning privacy via network-off and accessibility via live contrast recomputation. "Prove it yourself" properties are a category-level novelty.
  • Data provenance as a visible feature. No tool I have found in this category distinguishes a capture time from Google's record of when the file arrived, and after the retraction above I state that as the limit of my searching rather than as a fact about the category.

Things I would cut if proposed

  • A cloud or upload version "for convenience." It would destroy the only real moat to save a step the client-side design already removes. Cut on sight.
  • Accounts, saved history, a dashboard. Retention theatre for a tool people use once or twice. It adds a database, a privacy liability and infra cost for no user value.
  • A paid tier, now. There is willingness to pay in the category, but monetising a trust-first free tool with zero users would poison distribution before it starts.
  • Telemetry "to understand users." It contradicts the thesis and would be the first thing a sceptical user checks for. Use consented manual sessions instead.

The honesty check fired, and the claim it was guarding lost

The first version of this section ended with a warning to itself: state the position as "the only free zero-install browser-based option," not "the only free option," because overclaiming is exactly what an interviewer fact-checks. Re-checking it on 18 August 2026 found that even the narrowed claim had gone. Takeout Fixer is free, runs entirely in the browser, uploads nothing, and repairs Google Photos metadata with ExifTool compiled to WebAssembly. It is the box this product claimed was empty.

The retraction is above, in the summary and in the Create cell. What did not survive: exclusivity. What did, checked in the rival's own shipped page and code rather than taken from its marketing, and stated as behaviour rather than as a boast:

  • It cannot put the date on the file. Its own copy says a browser cannot set file creation dates on disk, and argues that importers read the embedded dates instead. That field is the entire symptom this product exists to repair: the thing people actually see is Finder showing today.
  • It works in Chromium only. It says so itself, because it edits photos in place and needs the File System Access API. Save the Dates runs anywhere, because it hands back a copy instead of writing into a folder.
  • It writes to your originals, and tells you to keep the export until you have checked the result. Nothing here is ever written to an original.
  • Its times are the local machine's clock, and it says travel photos may therefore be hours out. Decision 1 is the opposite bet, taken before there was anyone to take it against.

It also loads PostHog. That is an ordinary choice and not a scandal, but "no telemetry, and you can prove it by turning your network off" is now a difference between two live products rather than a promise made into an empty room.

What this cost, and what I would not do about it

Losing an exclusivity claim is the cheapest thing that happened here, because the claim was never the moat. The uncomfortable part is the temptation it creates: the obvious way to match a tool that edits your folder in place is to edit the folder in place, and that is the one trade I will not make. It is Chromium only, it writes to originals, and it still cannot set the file date. Taking it would spend three of the four differences above to save one unzip.

There is a real tension left unresolved, and it belongs here rather than in a footnote. Apple Photos and Immich both import a folder. This tool hands back zips. Removing the unzip at the front while leaving one at the back is a smaller win than it sounds, and I do not have a way to close it that does not cost the moat.
